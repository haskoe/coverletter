# **Abstract** {#abstract}

**Background:** Chronic kidney disease (CKD) mineral disturbances are associated with  increased mortality. Dietary phosphorus restrictions are burdensome and it remains uncertain when it is beneficial. Studies in CKD stages 3-4 suggest the kidneys manage to excrete excess dietary phosphorus questioning the necessity for early restrictions. Guidelines suggest that excessive calcium intake may be as harmful as insufficient intake, yet current calcium guidelines are based on two balance studies. Finally, clinical dietary tools like 24-hour recall are prone to bias, highlighting the need for a more accurate alternative that is feasible.

**Purpose:** To assess the correlation between the creatinine clearance (CRCL) and the apparent balances (dietary intake minus 24-hour urinary excretion) and excretion to intake ratios of calcium and phosphorus. Additionally, to examine the correlation and methodological agreement between an image based dietary assessment method (IBDA) and a weighted food record (WFR) for phosphorus, calcium, sodium and protein intake.

**Methods:** This observational study included 32 non-dialysis participants (CKD stage 4-5). Apparent balances and ratios for phosphorus and calcium were calculated using CrCl from timed 24-hour urine collection as a marker for renal function. Dietary intake was assessed using 3 day WFR and 1 day IBDA. Methodological agreement and correlations and subsequent sodium balances were compared between the IBDA and the corresponding single day of WFR using Bland-Altman analysis and Spearman’s rank correlation(ρ).

**Results:** no significant correlations were found between CrCl and the apparent balances/ratios of phosphorus and calcium across both methods (ρ ranging from \-0.11 to 0.31) (p-values ranging from 0.09 to 0.90). A subgroup revealed a significant positive sodium balance across both methods in participants at CRCL \<20ml/min (n=8) vs \>20ml/min (n=22) (p=0.008 for IBDA and 0.0179 for WFR)

The IBDA correlated strongly and highly significantly with the WFR across all nutrients (ρ ranging from 0.71 to 0.81, p= \<0,0001 for all). Bland-Altman Plots demonstrated systemic underreporting by IBDA phosphorus, protein and sodium alongside a wide limit of agreements (LoA) and high individual variation. Conversely for calcium IBDA illustrated a high but consistent bias  (-138mg) with observation clustered tightly around the bias despite wide LoA (-500 to \+200 mg) driven by outliers

**Conclusion:** Apparent calcium and phosphorus balance were not associated with declining CrCl, whereas sodium retention occurred below 20ml/min. Large individual variations prevent the IBDA from interchangeably replacing the WFR for assessing sodium, phosphorus and protein intake. However due to consistent bias IBDA holds potential for assessing calcium intake if a correction factor is applied.

# **Introduction**  {#introduction}

It is estimated that approximately 10% of the Danish population suffers from chronic kidney disease (CKD). Diabetes and hypertension are the most common causes for the development of CKD [(*Kronisk Nyresygdom*, u.å.)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?IvPeDp) [(*Forebyggelse \- Nyreforeningen*, 2014\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hXEQEd). In CKD, disturbances in the calcium-phosphate balance frequently arise resulting in *Chronic Kidney Disease – Mineral and Bone Disorder* (CKD-MBD). CKD-MBD is manifested by an abnormal metabolism of calcium, phosphate, parathyroid hormone (PTH) or vitamin D, abnormal bone turnover, and vascular calcifications [(S. Moe et al., 2006\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ZmNMCO). Vascular calcifications, in CKD stage 3-4 is the leading cause of mortality accounting for 40-50% of death and occur at a higher rate compared to individuals with a normal renal function [(Yu et al., 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Jtbs7P). 

Dietary counselling is one of the interventions used to manage disturbances in calcium-phosphate balance in CKD. (Elung-Jensen & Kristensen, 2024\)[(Evenepoel et al., 2024\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?o1Xj7f). However because phosphorus is found in the majority of the food sources consumed  dietary restrictions can be demanding for the patient. Current guidelines recommend adjusting the dietary phosphate intake according to the phosphate concentration [(Ikizler et al., 2020\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zWqKf5). In contrast to earlier guidelines where an upper limit of 800-1000 mg from the diet was recommended for managing hyperphosphatemia. [(Brandi et al., 2011\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ws7SMj). 

The consensus guidelines from *European Renal Nutrition* and *European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism* highlight that excessive intake of calcium may be as harmful as insufficient intake (Evenpoel et. al, 2023\) [(Evenepoel et al., 2024\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Auu7XC). It is currently suggested that Evidence regarding calcium balance in patients with CKD is based on only two metabolic balance studies conducted in small populations [(Hill et al., 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?q6rphe) [(Spiegel & Brady, 2012\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?YnBO2m)

The prevalence of hyperphosphatemia increases progressively with advancing CKD severity with both elevated phosphorus Fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) being associated with CVD incidence and mortality [(Rastogi et al., 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?QkKGpp) [(Cozzolino et al., 2019\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?p9z0Kc) However the benefits of early dietary phosphorus restriction is less clear. Studies with CKD stage 3-4 suggests that the kidneys maintain a capacity to compensate by increasing fractional excretion of phosphorus when challenged by a phosphorus load [(Salomo et al., 2017\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?cH82Pl) [(Selamet et al., 2016\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hAqi6s). Interestingly dietary studies have demonstrated a reduction in 24-hour urinary phosphorus excretion by dietary phosphorus reduction or substituting animal products with plant based products. However the shift in 24-hour urinary excretion and corresponding changes in plasma are not consistent  [(S. M. Moe et al., 2011\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mh2BPH) [(Isakova et al., 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?RzJJ57) [(Misella Hansen et al., 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?7j6AbB).  In contrast to hyperphosphatemia, the link between phosphorus excretion, the deterioration of kidney function and the incidence of cardiovascular disease (CVD) is less clear. Research indicates a weak association between dietary intake measured by phosphorus excretion, mortality, and serum phosphorus [(Selamet et al., 2016\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?sHUSU3). Some studies indicate an association between urinary phosphorus and CVD in early stages of CKD (stage 2\) [(Donat‐Vargas et al., 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?JsDOoI). High Dietary phosphorus intake has been linked to increased mortality in hemodialysis patients [(Noori et al., 2010\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?AXWJ6C). Less is known about pre-dialysis patients with CKD stage 4-5 since no studies have assessed the balance within that patient group. It is therefore relevant to investigate apparent calcium-phosphate balance in patients with CKD stage 4-5.

**Dietary assessment methods**

In clinical practice it is difficult to assess the dietary intake for these patients. 24-hour dietary recall is the most commonly used method in clinical practice but the method relies on the patient's memory and for this reason is highly susceptible to recall bias. While the weighted food record is considered as the gold standard for quantifying dietary intake, requiring patients to weigh all food and beverages consumed is often unfeasible especially for the older population. Furthermore  the method often requires multiple days to give the most reliable picture of the habitual dietary intake[(Ortega, 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?pOooBZ).  Patients also need access to a weight scale and it becomes inconvenient when a participant is consuming a meal outside of home or at a restaurant. Patients report a lower satisfaction rate compared to other methods such as image based dietary assessment[(Martin et al., 2008\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mLYqHo). Over the years image based dietary assessment method has been frequently used whereby patients photograph every food and fluid item consumed. This method could be a potential new solution for accessing dietary intake. When analyzed by a registered dietitian (RD) this method demonstrates a good correlation and ranking agreement when validated against weighted records and a higher satisfaction reported by participants[(Martin et al., 2008\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CTrLoE). While the image based methods have previously been used to assess the daily intake of protein and energy, the method has not been evaluated for phosphorus and calcium intake for patients with CKD stage 4-5.

## **Kidney physiological functions** {#kidney-physiological-functions}

The kidneys are essential organs for maintaining homeostasis by regulating fluid levels, concentration of electrolytes, acid-base balance, and maintaining blood pressure. This is done by filtration, reabsorption, secretion, and excretion. The kidneys are also responsible for activating vitamin D by hydroxylating the vitamin in the proximal tubule, producing erythropoietin needed for red blood cell formation, and contributing to blood pressure control through hormonal and paracrine regulation  [(Boron & Boulpaep, 2017\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?eFUdUl) [(Giebisch et al., 2017a, s. 722\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?szijlH).

## **The nephron** {#the-nephron}

Each kidney contains roughly 1 million nephrons. The nephron is the functional unit responsible for filtering the plasma and forming urine. A nephron is made up of a glomerulus, and a tubule. The glomerulus where the filtration of the plasma to ultrafiltrate occurs and the tubule is where the urine is transported and the part that determines the urine composition through secretion and reabsorption. The tubular system consists of several segments, each with distinct epithelial cell types: The proximal convoluted tubule (PCT), the proximal straight tubule (PST), loop of Henle (consisting of a thin descending limb (tDLH), thick descending limb, thin ascending limb (tALH) and thick ascending limb (TAL)), distal tubule and the collecting ducts [(Giebisch et al., 2017a, s. 722–729)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?jOdqHH).

## **Phosphate transport in the healthy kidney** {#phosphate-transport-in-the-healthy-kidney}

Phosphate homeostasis is tightly regulated by the kidneys in healthy individuals, maintaining plasma concentration within a range of roughly 0.75-1.5 mmol/L. More than ten times the total extracellular phosphate is filtered a day, and approximately 80-95% of this is reabsorbed in normal conditions [(Giebisch et al., 2017b, s. 785\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?YRgQA8). Even small increments in plasma phosphate led to increased phosphate excretion, due to a low renal threshold for phosphate reabsorption. This illustrates that phosphate excretion is very efficient  [(Giebisch et al., 2017b, s. 786\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?i7PK8Q). The vast majority of the filtered phosphate (80-95%) is reabsorbed in the proximal tubule (80-95%). Three apical cotransporters are known to play a role in phosphate transport: NaPi-IIa , NaPi-IIc and PiT-2. These transporters move phosphate into the cell alongside with Na⁺, via secondary active transport utilizing the electrochemical gradient generated by the basolateral Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase. NaPi-IIa and PiT-2 are electrogenic while the NaPi-IIc is electroneutral [(Giebisch et al., 2017a, s. 786\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?8wN5PF). 

Dietary phosphorus intake affects these transporters: Phosphate depletion upregulates the expression of Na+/Pi transporter favoring reabsorption. Conversely excessive dietary phosphorus intake downregulates these transporters, enhancing excretion. Additionally, luminal pH affects the phosphate transporters NaPi-IIa and NaPi-IIc. The activity of these two transporters falls tremendously when the pH is reduced from 8 to 6.5, whereas PiT-2 is less sensitive to pH. The diminished activity of NaPi-IIa and NaPi-IIc results in phosphaturia, which is thought to be a physiological response due to phosphate that works as a tiltrateable acid buffer for excess H+ in acidic conditions [(Blaine et al., 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?LFwNQh) [(Giebisch et al., 2017b, s. 824\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?NxjybZ). Worth noticing that the phosphate buffer is most abundant intracellular, and may become relevant during states of severe acidosis. Although the HCO3- buffer is the main acid buffer. Phosphate homeostasis is also under the influence of several hormones; fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23) and parathyroid hormone (PTH), of which FGF23 are the most important [(Giebisch et al., 2017d, s. 787\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ihzkWt) .

## **Calcium transport in the healthy kidney:** {#calcium-transport-in-the-healthy-kidney:}

In plasma Ca2+ exists in three different forms: approximately 40% is bound to albumin, 15% of Ca2+ is complexed with anions (phosphate, citrate, sulfate), and 45% is ionized which is the  physiologically active form. The calcium bound to albumin is not filterable through the glomerulus (Giebisch et al., 2017c, p 787\) [(Giebisch et al., 2017d, s. 787\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GIZtv1). Acid-base status influences Total serum calcium levels due to the competitive binding of H+ and Ca2+ to albumin. In alkalosis, fewer H+ ions are bound allowing more calcium to bind to albumin, lowering free ionized Ca2+. Acidosis has the opposite effect. While total serum calcium is normally in the range of 2.2-2.6 mmol/L, the ionized Ca2+ is regulated within a narrower range (1.2-1.4 mmol/L) by the kidneys, and several hormonal factors [(Blaine et al., 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FyiSCa). 

In the proximal tubule Ca2+ is reabsorbed primarily paracellularly, following water and Na+ transport through solvent drag and passive diffusion. A smaller fraction (10-15%) of the reabsorption, is thought to be by active transcellular transport [(Blaine et al., 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?jmr9Xb)

In the thick ascending limb, Ca2+ is reabsorbed paracellularly, driven by a lumen-positive transepithelial potential. This voltage is generated by: 

* Na+/K+/Cl− transporter (NKCC2) moving Cl− into the cell.   
* The K+ (ROMK) channel, recycling K+ into the lumen. 

Together these two mechanisms make the lumen less negative, creating a favorable lumen to blood gradient for paracellular Ca2+ and magnesium reabsorption. Additionally, a calcium sensing receptor (CaSR) located in the basolateral membrane, senses the amount of Ca2+. Low Ca2+ concentration inhibits the sensor, whereas a high concentration increases the activity of the sensor. CaSR modulates  the expression of tight junction proteins that regulate the permeability of the paracellular pathway (Ibid., 2015). Thus, when the sensor is activated, more tight junctions are expressed, and more calcium is excreted in the urine. 

In contrast to earlier segments, the distal tubule reabsorbs Ca2+ via a transcellular pathway in three steps (Ibid., 2015): 

 Ca2+ is taken up in the cell by an apical calcium channel known as “the transient receptor vanilloid 5” is found.   
Within the cell, Ca2+ is buffered by a protein (calbindin-D28k). This is an important step for cytosolic transport, and to keep the intracellular Ca2+ concentration low  keeping the favorable Ca2+ concentration gradient.   
 In the last step, Ca2+ is extruded across the basolateral membrane by a Na+/Ca2+ exchanger (NCX1) and a Ca2+ ATPase pump (PMCA). 

High luminal calcium leads to increased diuresis. A likely explanation could be that high extracellular calcium seems to inhibit aquaporin expression. Furthermore, studies indicate High luminal Ca2+ acting via the CaSR may inhibit the ADH induced aquaporin trafficking [(Noda & Sasaki, 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SdOVnS). 

Calcium homeostasis is tightly controlled by a hormone known as parathyroid hormone (PTH) which will be described in the following section.

## **Parathyroid hormone (PTH)** {#parathyroid-hormone-(pth)}

PTH is a peptide hormone secreted by the chief cells in the parathyroid gland. The hormone is a major regulator of extracellular calcium levels and to a lesser extent phosphate and responds to fluctuations in ionized Ca2+ (sensed by a calcium receptor)  and vitamin D. It regulates Ca2+ and phosphate by:

* Increasing Ca2+ reabsorption in the distal tubule and increasing the renal phosphorus excretion by downregulating  the apical NAPi-IIa  and NAPi-IIc in the proximal tubule.  
* PTH stimulates osteoblast cells to release molecules including macrophage colony stimulating factor (M-CSF) and Receptor Activator Of Nuclear Factor Kappa-B Ligand (RANKL). These molecules promote osteoclastic precursors to proliferate and differentiate into multinucleated osteoclast, which begins bone resorption. The resorption releases  Ca2+ into the bloodstream from hydroxyapatite crystals [(Barrett & Barrett, 2017, s. 1057\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SX9Oom)  
* Finally PTH stimulates 1a-hydroxylase activity, the enzyme responsible for hydroxylation of  25(OH)D into 1,25(OH)₂D the active vitamin D form.[(Barrett & Barrett, 2017, s. 1062\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?hehdFH)

## **Fibroblast growth factor 23 (FGF23)** {#fibroblast-growth-factor-23-(fgf23)}

FGF23 is a major phosphaturic hormone secreted primarily by osteocytes and osteoblastic cells.  To maintain normophosphatemia  in response to increasing phosphorus levels the hormone binds to the FGFR1c receptor and to the co-receptor, α-klotho, that increases the receptor affinity. This binding initiates endocytosis and degradation of the phosphate cotransporters NAPi-IIa and NAPi-IIc, increasing phosphate excretion. Additionally FGF23 reduces the concentration of the active vitamin D 1,25(OH)₂D, and inhibits PTH secretion. CKD patients have a reduced level of  a-klotho, and an elevated FGF23 concentration which is probably a contributor to the FGF23 resistance seen in these patients. It is also recognized that the hormone is an independent risk factor for left ventricular hypertrophy, heart failure and increased mortality for CKD patients [(Diniz & Frazão, 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Z6ofHn). 

## **Chronic kidney disease:** {#chronic-kidney-disease:}

 Chronic kidney disease is a chronic condition characterized by a decline in eGFR. The term is used for deteriorating kidney function regardless of the underlying cause.  As filtration declines, waste products and toxic substances accumulate, and markers such as proteinuria or albuminuria are often present in CKD. 

Although the initial mechanisms triggering renal injury are not entirely understood and vary depending on the underlying disease, The general  pathophysiology involves a final common pathway characterized by the progressive loss of nephrons. The remaining nephrons respond by hyperfiltration, which damages the remaining nephrons further, eventually leading to proteinuria. This is accompanied by an Increased activation of the renin-angio-tensin-aldosterone system (RAAS). The release of angiotensin II (ANGII) leads to vasoconstriction, elevated intraglomerular pressure and increased filtration fraction, which further exacerbate the damage. ANGII upregulates other proteins that contribute to matrix formation and scarring in the glomeruli and tubules, leading to tubulointerstitial fibrosis. Due to the connection between the glomeruli and the tubules, injury to the glomeruli will spread to the tubules, and the interstitium causing tubulointerstitial injury [(Yaqoop & Ashman, 2020, s. 1393–1394)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?qTRGKp).

CKD is classified into five stages depending on the GFR level and the amount of albuminuria see figure 1\. In clinical practice eGFR is used instead [(Stevens et al., 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SqsTfo). 

Stage 1: the patient has an eGFR of 90ml/min or above. Patients are typically asymptomatic but there is a mild damage to the kidneys.

Stage 2 the patient has an eGFR between 60-89 ml/min at this stage the phosphorus levels are normal but elevated FGF23 starts to be present.

 Stage 3 (subdivided into 3a: 45-59 and 3b: 30-44ml/min) At this stage altered balance in mineral homeostasis starts to occur. Active vitamin D declines while FGF23, PTH, rises to keep serum phosphate within normal levels.  Anemia starts to be pronounced as well. At this stage some patients may experience edema and fatigue [(Diniz & Frazão, 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GsYeAE).

Stage 4 (eGFR 15-29 ml/min)

Plasma phosphate increases as the compensatory increase in FGF23 and PTH, can no longer offset the loss of nephrons. This combination of hyperphosphatemia, hypocalcemia, vitamin D deficiency and secondary hyperparathyroidism increases the risk of calcifications and renal osteodystrophy. Metabolic acidosis starts to be present as well [(Melamed et al., 2016\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5YaEuI). 

Stage 5 (\<15ml/min)

also known as end stage renal disease (ESDR). The eGFR is \<15 hyperphosphatemia and   CKD-MBD prevalence is increasing.   The accumulation of uremic toxins (such as urea) reaches levels that affect the organs, causing uremic symptoms such as nausea, itchy skin and neurological changes. When these symptoms can no longer be managed, Renal replacement therapy such as dialysis or  transplantation can become necessary to remove excessive waste which the kidneys are unable to. Dialysis is also indicated when hyperphosphatemia and hyperkalemia cannot be managed through diet and medications [(Rivara et al., 2017\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?a5QRSD).

## **Phosphorus and calcium balance in CKD.** {#phosphorus-and-calcium-balance-in-ckd.}

As Chronic kidney disease progresses, phosphate accumulation is common due to the remaining nephrons in the kidney, being unable to excrete phosphate efficiently. This may eventually lead to hyperphosphatemia [(Yaqoop & Ashman, 2020, s. 1394\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kPupMk). The remaining nephrons will compensate by exerting a higher fractional urinary phosphorus excretion, in order to maintain normal plasma levels. Early micropuncture studies done on isolated rat nephrons indicate that this compensation occurs via reduced phosphate reabsorption in the proximal tubule, resulting in increased phosphate concentrations in the tubular fluid [(Bank et al., 1978\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?pLYmhS). Concurrently intestinal absorption may not be affected: Hill et al found a stool phosphorus content of roughly 50% of the dietary intake in patients with CKD stage 3-4, suggesting the absorption remains intact [(Hill et al., 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?sK4lUO). 

In the early stages of CKD, FGF23 rises even before hyperphosphatemia and secondary hyperparathyroidism develop, highlighting the major role the hormone plays in the maintenance of phosphate homeostasis [(Isakova et al., 2011\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ncQofc). A negative correlation between FGF23 and active vitamin D 1.25 dihydroxycholecalciferol is seen, suggesting FGF23 may suppress the active hydroxylating of vitamin D [(Shigematsu et al., 2004\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?fj4lx4). The decline in active vitamin D results in reduced intestinal absorption of calcium, lowering calcium levels. Hypocalcemia is sensed by a calcium receptor known as CaSR in the parathyroid gland. The parathyroid gland secrete PTH to restore calcium balance. PTH stimulates 1-a hydroxylase activating vitamin D and increases calcium reabsorption and in addition increases phosphate excretion. However, as CKD progresses, elevated PTH and FGF23 fails to maintain phosphate and active vitamin D is inhibited by elevated FGF 23 levels. This leads to a vicious cycle, in which low calcium and vitamin D continue to stimulate PTH release, eventually resulting in secondary hyperparathyroidism (sHPT) [(Komaba, 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xGAn0b).  

For dietary guidelines, the current recommendation is to adjust phosphorus intake to keep plasma phosphorus within the normal range [(Ikizler et al., 2020\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Xnf4OI) . Older guidelines suggested  limiting the maximum phosphorus intake to 800-1000 mg a day in the presence of hyperphosphatemia [(Brandi et al., 2011\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?dzMRj9). 

Regarding calcium balance. Studies are limited. It has been suggested that urinary calcium excretion falls as early as stage 1 and markedly decreased at stage 4 [(Guo et al., 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?FwbAIc) . Both hypercalcemia and hypocalcemia are seen among late-stage CKD patients. Spiegel and Brady conducted a metabolic balance study with a crossover RCT design, comparing healthy individuals and patients with CKD stage 3-4. The participants were exposed to a calcium intake of 800 mg and 2000 mg a day. The CKD patients had a marked reduction in urinary calcium excretion compared to the control group (83 vs 272 mg of mean urinary calcium excretion) but maintained a near neutral balance on 800 mg calcium diet, likely due to impaired intestinal absorption resulting in increased fecal excretion. However the CKD participants failed to compensate by increased fecal excretion at a calcium intake of 2000 mg/day and ended up having a more positive balance  compared to the control group. [(Spiegel & Brady, 2012\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?71iEL8).Hill et al also found similar results in CKD 3-4 regarding calcium balance showing a near neutral balance of 800-1000 mg but increasing calcium either through diet or by supplements produced a largely positive balance [(Hill et al., 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?G30i9r). KDIGO recommends that CKD patients should aim for 800-1000 mg and not exceed 1500 mg (diets and supplements included) [(Stevens et al., 2024\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GIIXjn). Although insufficient calcium intake has shown to increase the risk of bone fractures, higher intakes increase the risk of vascular calcification. Therefore the optimal intake remains to be determined.

## **Vascular calcification** {#vascular-calcification}

A serious consequence of disturbances in calcium and phosphate metabolism is vascular calcifications. The pathophysiology is poorly understood. Calcification manifests both in the intimal layer and medial layer of the arterial wall while the medial being most common in CKD [(Dube et al., 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Wgi7sV). it has been suggested that several factors potentially play a role:

elevated calcium-phosphate product (Ca x P) elevates the risk of precipitation of hydroxyapatite crystals, in the arterial wall of soft tissues and organs. The vascular smooth muscle cells can undergo transdifferentiation to an osteoblastic phenotype. These cells are not terminally differentiated and are able to dedifferentiate to other cell types depending on the environment. Hyperphosphatemia and hypercalcemia seem to augment the differentiation. It has been proposed this occurs, due to upregulation of osteogenic genes including runx2 [(Dube et al., 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?48hGu8). These osteoblast-like cells release extracellular vesicles, and within these vesicles’ calcium and phosphate forms hydroxyapatite. Apoptosis of smooth muscle cells may play a role by forming apoptotic bodies, thereby promoting nucleation sites for crystal formation. 

In addition to the vesicle-based mineralization, calciprotein particles (CPP) have also been suggested to play a role. CPP are soluble complexes of calcium and phosphate, stabilized by a hepatic protein such as fetuin-a. In a healthy state these primary (amorphous) CPP clear excess calcium and phosphate. However, these particles can develop into secondary CPP. Secondary CPP plays a role in the calcification [(Magagnoli & Cozzolino, 2025\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?uS2obM). Fetuin-A is thought to suppress the transition from primary to secondary CPPs. In CKD, levels of Fetuin A are often reduced.

## **Pressure natriuresis in CKD and dietary sodium recommendation** {#pressure-natriuresis-in-ckd-and-dietary-sodium-recommendation}

Sodium (𝑁𝑎+) is the primary osmotically active solute in the extracellular fluid. The kidney maintains the extracellular fluid by regulating the amount of 𝑁𝑎+ in the urine.  

Under normal physiological conditions, the glomeruli filters roughly 25000 mmol of  𝑁𝑎+ per day. Because water passively follows, the osmotic gradient generated by  𝑁𝑎+ reabsorption across the tubule cells, even short term (minutes) alterations highly affects the systemic fluid volume [(Giebisch et al., 2017c, s. 754\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?fy2fmR)

Sodium is under tight regulation by a steroid hormone known as aldosterone. In response to a depletion in extracellular volume, aldosterone is secreted from the adrenal cortex and increases the reabsorption of 𝑁𝑎+, mainly in the distal tubule and collecting ducts. Aldosterone binds to mineralocorticoid receptors on principal cells and upregulates an epithelial sodium channel (ENAC) and the  𝑁𝑎+ K+ atapase pump. [(Barrett, 2017, s. 1027\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?L9FuEa)

Renal perfusion also directly regulates sodium excretion; The mechanism is known as pressure natriuresis [(Baek & Kim, 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?tubjjC). When blood pressure rises due to increased extracellular volume, the renal arterial pressure increases. The kidney responds by increasing excretion of 𝑁𝑎+ , which in turn lowers extracellular volume, and blood pressure. 

As CKD progresses, salt-sensitive hypertension frequently develops mainly because the pressure natriuresis mechanism becomes impaired. In salt-sensitive hypertension, there is a rightward shift in the pressure natriuresis curve meaning a higher blood pressure is needed to excrete the same amount of 𝑁𝑎+ . This contributes to 𝑁𝑎+ retention and hypertension [(Baek & Kim, 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Bhs79D)

KDIGO recommends a maximum intake of \<2 g of sodium a day (\<5 g of sodium chloride) [(Ikizler et al., 2020\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?VRnTsN). The average intake of sodium chloride in Denmark is 11 grams (4.4 grams of sodium) for men and 8 grams (3.2 grams of sodium) for women [(Nadelmann Pedersen, 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?P1OvfZ). The majority of sodium is found in bread and bakery products, meat and fish products and ready meals such as pizza. A meta analysis by Garofalo  et al consisting of 11 RCT studies and an overall 738 CKD patients(CKD stage 1-4) found a mean reduction of 4.9 mmhg in  systolic blood pressure and 2.3 mmhg in diastolic blood pressure in the group with a low sodium intake compared to the high sodium intake group  Additionally, significant reductions in proteinuria (-0.39g/day) and albuminuria (-0.05g day) were observed. The specific sodium intakes were not established, but the urinary sodium excretion for low and high groups were 104 mEq/day and 179 mEq/day respectively [(Garofalo et al., 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1o8TIJ).   

## **Phosphorus food sources** {#phosphorus-food-sources}

Phosphorus is present in most of the food items consumed. Phosphorus exists both in organic and inorganic forms. Organic phosphorus is found in animal and plant-based products, whereas inorganic phosphate is added to food known as food additives. The bioavailability of organic phosphorus from animal products is substantially higher than for plant-based products and the bioavailability [(St-Jules et al., 2017a)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?inag2f). Plant-based phosphorus is bound to phytate in food and as humans lack the intestinal enzyme phytase required to break down phytate, gastrointestinal absorption is limited. This Means for an equivalent amount of phosphorus intake from plant based compared to animal based foods less phosphorus will be absorbed from the plant based [(Iqbal et al., 1994\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?zbaU4c). The situation seems even more critical for inorganic phosphorus that has an absorption close to 100%; Benini et al observed that products containing phosphorus additives may contain 70% more phosphorus than products without additives [(Benini et al., 2011\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?HY7pkb).  Phosphorus content in foods correlates strongly with the protein content. Animal products with a high phosphorus content include dairy products, meat, fish, egg yolk and poultry. Plant products with high content include legumes, nuts, oats, chocolate and bread [(St-Jules et al., 2017b)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?OOLzGE). 

KDIGO guidelines recommend a diet containing plant-based foods, while limiting animal-based products and ultra-processed foods [(Stevens et al., 2024\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kR9XG6). Substitution of animal products with vegetarian and limiting phosphorus additives could potentially lead to a slight reduction in FGF23, fractional urinary phosphorus excretion and serum phosphorus for CKD stage 3-4 [(Misella Hansen et al., 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?AtkZHd), [(S. M. Moe et al., 2011\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lIjnzp) [(Salomo et al., 2019\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?2EuC6T). This suggests that dietary guidance in the presence of hyperphosphatemia should not only focus on the quantity of dietary phosphorus but also the source and bioavailability.

In summary: limiting phosphorus additives and substituting some of the animal phosphorus sources with vegetarian sources can potentially reduce the serum phosphorus, FGF23 and urinary phosphorus excretion. Attenuating deposition of calcium phosphorus in soft tissues and the metabolic consequences leading to mineral bone disorder.

## **Protein recommendation** {#protein-recommendation}

The average protein intake for adults in Denmark is roughly 1.2 g/kg bodyweight [(Nadelmann Pedersen, 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?sAnXkQ). Because amino acids cannot be stored in the body, excess protein is catabolised into waste products such as urea and other toxins, in the state of progressive CKD, these toxins gradually starts to accumulate, therefore dietary protein restriction is recommended to ameliorate the uremic symptoms that patients experience. KDIGO guidelines recommend a protein intake of 0.8 g/kg body weight [(Stevens et al., 2024\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?dv2Vg2). A cochrane review by Hahn et al (2020) included 10 studies examining a low protein intake of 0.6 g/kg body weight compared to a normal protein intake of \>0.8g/kg body weight in patients with stage 3a and 3b CKD. The author found no significant differences in mortality, the probability of progressing to end-stage kidney disease or the rate of GFR decline between the groups. Additionally, eight studies were included comparing a very low protein diet (VLPD) of 0.3-0.4g/kg body weight per day against a low or normal in patients with CKD stage 4-5. Although a little or no difference was seen in overall mortality, A VLPD  probably reduces the number of patients that progressed to end stage kidney disease [(Hahn et al., 2020\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?S4BaDr) .  However, a VLPD raises concerns regarding an increased risk of malnutrition and protein energy wasting, since the intake is markedly lower than the average requirement to maintain neutral nitrogen balance[(Geirsdóttir & Pajari, 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UUtnH8). A strategy to counteract it may be supplementation with nitrogen-free ketoacid analogues. These ketoacids lack an amino group (-NH2)  but can still serve as a substrate for the synthesis of essential amino acids by transamination reactions. This pathway increases the pool of essential amino acids without accumulation of toxic end products like urea [(Garneata et al., 2016\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?TmmbDj). 

The source of proteins has also been suggested to play a role. Substituting animal proteins with plant proteins can potentially modulate renal blood flow. While it is indicated, animal protein induce dilation of the afferent arteriole plant proteins may counteract that dilation, thus reducing intraglomerular pressure and hyperfiltration [(Kontessis et al., 1990\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PN3Fpd). A RCT found a higher proportion of plant proteins to animal proteins may reduce GFR decline [(Podadera-Herreros et al., 2022\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GzE9El). But due to the limited amount of studies more research is needed. Since phosphorus and protein content correlate strongly in food, limiting dietary protein is effective at reducing phosphorus intake.

# **Dietary assessment methods** {#dietary-assessment-methods}

In this section the dietary assessment methods used in this master thesis to gather dietary data will be described. 

## **Weighted food records** {#weighted-food-records}

The dietary weighted record (WFR) is widely referred to as the “gold standard” for dietary assessment. This method is frequently used to validate other dietary assessment methods in dietary research[(Ortega, 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ko5Ch9). WFR is a prospective, open-ended assessment method, where the subject is required to weigh all food and beverages consumed over a specified period of time. The data collected does not rely on the individual memory as the items are recorded at the point of consumption, thereby eliminating the risk of recall bias. Additionally because the exact weight is quantified on a weight scale, the method does not depend on subjective portion size estimations.

To maximize data accuracy, it is recommended that subjects give a detailed description of the brand name, specific recipes, and the cooking method. Furthermore multiple days of weighing are recommended to catch habitual dietary patterns, as fewer days will likely not be representative. The WFR has been validated by comparing the dietary intake assessed by the WFR against objective biomarkers, such as urinary nitrogen excretion showing a strong correlation above 0.7; In contrast food frequency questionnaires (FFQ) and 24-hour dietary recalls typically show small to medium correlations [(Bingham et al., 1995\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?isHgsW). The WFR has also been shown to be useful for assessing phosphorus intake [(Livingstone et al., 1990\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?pPJ7Qr) and calcium intake [(Trautvetter et al., 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?anCZoo) when validated against urinary excretion. 

Despite being referred to as the gold standard, energy intake assessed by WFR compared with energy expenditure assessed by double labelled, tends to be underreported as much as 20% [(Seale & Rumpler, 1997\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?GW6mSu) [(Livingstone et al., 1990\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?yND03U). The underreporting can be due to several reasons, including social desirability bias– Where participants tend to underreport unhealthy food, and overreport healthy food. The cooking method matters as well, it is impossible to obtain 100% accuracy when accounting for evaporation of water after cooking or frying food. Finally, the analysis of the WFR by the RD of the food intake reported is prone to inaccuracy as well due to food databases only giving an estimate, and they differ between different countries.

## **24-hour recall supported by photos** {#24-hour-recall-supported-by-photos}

The 24-hour dietary recall is a retrospective dietary assessment method. In clinical practice, a RD conducts an interview asking the patient to describe and quantify the intake of food and fluids consumed prior to or during the day before the interview. This includes every meal and snacks from breakfast to dinner [(Salvador Castell, 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xwdw0B). However, dietary recall is prone to recall bias referred to as “memory bias” which can manifest as forgetting items, reporting non consumed items or reporting or substituting non consumed items with consumed items. [(Vanderkooy et al., 2025\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?mFUtyr). Several studies indicate that dietary recall underreports the intake of energy and macronutrients when evaluated against a reference method like 7 days WFR   [(Jayawardena, 2016\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?aoVIDa). Additionally, energy intake reported by participants conducted by a 24-hour recall is prone to underreporting when compared with energy expenditure assessed by indirect calorimetry. 

However, adding photographs to support the 24-hour recall has been shown to minimize the underreporting [(Gemming et al., 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?cr1HHP) . This image-assisted approach provides the RD with visualized portion sizes thereby reducing the reliance on the patients subjective estimation. Multiple studies evaluating energy and macronutrient intake demonstrate a good correlation between image-based dietary assessment and WFR when the photographs are reviewed and analyzed by an experienced registered dietitian.. Additionally participants report a higher satisfaction rate compared to weighted records. [(Williamson et al., 2003\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CHv4aO) [(Martin et al., 2008\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PpEQSX) [(Naaman et al., 2021\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?okZeBU).

However, despite its advantages the limitations. The quantification of the portion size relies on the subjective perception of the RD. The accuracy depends on the compliance of the participant; If a participant forgets to photograph a meal. When reference pictures are missing the quantification relies on guessing the portion weight.. The participant must be well trained to capture pictures at correct angles. Finally, identifying hidden ingredients can be difficult, especially in a cooked dish.

# **Methodology** {#methodology}

## **CPH-MBD cohort** {#cph-mbd-cohort}

This master thesis was conducted as a dietary sub study within the observational cohort study “CPH-MBD”. Both the main and substudy are conducted at the department of nephrology at Herlev hospital, in collaboration with the University of Copenhagen . The aim of the main study is to explore factors that may predict or associate with the development of bones and cardiovascular diseases in 1000 patients with  CKD stage 4-5. 50 patients were included in the substudy. The aim of the dietary sub study was to assess the correlation between calcium and phosphorus balance and the stage of kidney disease measured by creatinine clearance in patients with CKD stage 4-5. In addition to that, the purpose was also to compare image based dietary assessment with dietary food weighted records. The study was registered at clinicaltrials.gov before the start of recruitment. Recruitment began 07-01-2026. The last participant was included in the substudy 29-05-2026. For the purpose of this master thesis the data analysis began 1st June. At this point, a total of 50 participants had been included in the substudy. Out of these 32 had completed their follow up meeting,  10 participants were still waiting for their follow up meeting. Recruitment of participants for both the main and the substudy was done by the primary investigator of the main study, and in addition to that the secretary of the department.  The information meeting, baseline meeting, data collection, data processing and data reporting was done by the primary investigator of the substudy.

**Pseudonymizing**

To secure data protection and maintain systematic workflow all of the screened and included participants were pseudoanonymized. After the inclusion the participants were given a number that follows the 100 system (1xx) This is done to make sure the patients are pseudonymized and to ensure systematic during the data collection.

## **Study timeline** {#study-timeline}

The timeline for the participants comprised two  consultations.

*  Information meeting: information about the study was presented, informed consent was obtained and participants received instructions from the RD on how to conduct weighted food records and photograph their food and beverages  
* Baseline visit: follow up three hour baseline visit. During this consultation blood samples were drawn, the completed 24 hour urine was delivered and the dietary records were delivered and reviewed together with the RD.

## **Image based assisted dietary recall** {#image-based-assisted-dietary-recall}

The photographs were reviewed together with the participant. Followed by an image assisted dietary recall. The purpose was to confirm the complete registration of all foods, fluid, beverages and snacks. Participants were also asked to clarify items that were difficult to identify from the photographs i.e. if ingredients were hidden or covered by sauce. The participants were additionally encouraged to ask questions regarding their registrations. 

For the participants who completed the three-day WFR, these records were reviewed together with the participant. Details were obtained regarding whether the food items were weighted (prepared or raw) and the exact amount of fluid/milk added to beverages including coffee, tea or fluid syrup. If a dish were consumed and the weight of the ingredients in raw state before preparation weren’t recorded, the participants were asked to recall the specific ingredients. This allowed the primary investigator to identify the major phosphorus and calcium sources, thereby increasing the accuracy. When food or beverages were recorded but without a corresponding weight the primary investigator assisted the participants in estimating the missing portion sizes. This was done using a combination of household measures (shown below), visual aids provided by the primary investigator and validated photographic series by DTU with a corresponding weight. Portion estimation were standardized as followed

* **fluid items**: number of cups whether it was a small, medium or large cup.   
* **Potatoes:** number of egg sized potatoes consumed.   
* **pasta, rice, grains or legumes**: an estimation in deciliter or cups.   
* **Meat,fish and poultry:** the  number of beef, chicken breast, salmon steak pieces and whether it was large, small or medium.

 The WFR plus the dietary recall was compared to the photographs plus the dietary recall in the following analysis. 

 **Dietary analysis and nutrient calculation**

The weighted dietary records and the photographs were documented in the nutrient calculation software vitakost (Vitakost Denmark). To maximize coverage of items multiple databases were used within including:  Fødevaredata, frida, 2025, version 5.4, Fødevaredatabanken, Fødevaredatabanken, forbrugerfødevarer, Danske standardopskrifter, Navamedic, Nutricia, Matvaretabellen (Norway), Composition of Foods Integrated Dataset (UK), Livsmedelsverket Livsmedelsdatabas (Sweden) and USDA National Nutrient Database for Standard Reference (USA). The following macro and micronutrient data were extracted from vitakost: Energy, protein, fat, carbohydrates, calcium, phosphorus, magnesium and sodium.

The weight of the food and beverages from the photographs were estimated by the primary investigator. No independent secondary reviewer was evaluating the photographs. Data from the WFR was typically entered into vitakost before the photographs were reviewed. To prevent the previously entered WFR weights from influencing the visual portion size estimation, the primary investigator evaluated the photographs independently. Dietary photographs from DTU were used as reference photographs to improve the accuracy for the portion size estimation [(Nielsen et al., u.å.)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?oknbJ4). However, the reference photographs are limited regarding the number of different dishes. Alternative estimation methods were applied when no reference photographs were available. 

In such cases the specific ingredients or food items were identified (eg. Number of almonds, pieces of meat, dried fruit, or other countable ingredients). Portion sizes were then estimated by using the standardized weight of the specific items available in vitakost (grams per almonds, potatoes,) This allows a more precise estimation for foods with countable components. 

For dishes the individual ingredients from the photographs were counted and estimated. 

To preserve independence between the two dietary assessment methods, data from the WFR were never used to replace missing data in the IBDA and vice versa In cases where a photograph was missing and no supplementary information was available from participant notes, or from the dietary recall the intake for the image based dietary method was classified as missing data. If a weighted record of an item were missing and if an estimated weight couldn’t be obtained by the following dietary recall, the food item was not registered in vitakost. 

In situations where the weighted food records were documented by photographs showing the  food or beverages placed on a weight scale, The investigator would obscure or disregard the visible weight display during portion size estimation in order to maintain blinding of the meal size estimation. 

**Missing data in Vitakost**

Incomplete Macro and micro nutrient data for food and beverages were occasionally encountered in the vitakost database. in such situations:

*  If a specific food item was not available eg a brand of a cheese or meat product. An item within the same food group with a comparable phosphorus and calcium content was selected.  
*  Meat, poultry and fish where the weight was recorded in prepared state and no data of the prepared item were available were documented by an estimated raw weight in vitakost. To estimate the raw weight before preparation DTU “Mål, vægt og portionsstørrelser” was used as a guideline [(Ygil, 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?8pdaV3).   
*  In cases where tea and coffee were consumed with water the items would be reported as “water” in vitakost. In case if there were no data regarding the ratio of water to fluid syrup a 4:1 was used.

**Method comparison**

For the three-day WFR,  macro and micronutrient values were calculated for each of the three days. In addition to that, an average was calculated across the three days. The 24 hour photograph data were compared to the last day of the three day weighted record. In the case the participant had photographs for multiple days (two or three days) the values for the individual days would be calculated and an average would be calculated as well. If photographic data were missing for the final WFR day but were available for day one and/or two. Comparison between the two dietary assessment methods were done using the corresponding days where both photographic and weighted records were available.

## **Measurements** {#measurements}

Blood samples

Blood was drawn from all participants using standardized vein puncture techniques.

Around 30 ml of blood was collected for routine analysis at baseline. These blood samples were analyzed immediately by the Department of Clinical Biochemistry.

24 hour Urine samples

All participants were instructed to collect urine over 24 hours at the visit the participants gave informed consent. A 24 hour urine sample does not rely on the volume of the urine which can lead to bias in a spot urine sample [(Robinson-Cohen et al., 2014\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Qsjtjw). In addition a 24 hour urine sample will take account for the diurnal variation of calcium and phosphorus which can affect a spot urine sample. Even when a spot urine sample is normalized for creatinine it does not seem to be very representative of a 24 hour urine sample.[(Robinson-Cohen et al., 2014\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?BlUQ6V). A limitation of the 24 hour urine sample is the time consumption and participant burden. To limit the participant burden, the urinary samples were collected once. However estimation of dietary phosphorus intake from a single 24 hour urine collection is prone to underestimation or overestimation for CKD patients, and multiple days may be needed to achieve a minimum reliability of \>75% [(Stremke et al., 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?avq4rW)

All urine samples are measured in mmol/l and were converted to mg per day based on the total 24-hour urine volume. To calculate the daily excretion in milligrams, the total amount of millimoles excreted per day was multiplied by the atomic weight of the respective ions in grams per mole (g/mole) shown in the following equation:

 mg/day=mmol/day X atomic weight (g/mol) The applied atomic weights were 23 g/mol for sodium, 31g/mol for phosphorus and 40.1g/mol for calcium [(Prohaska et al., 2022\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lEfiC7). For example 1 mmol of sodium is 23 mg. 10 mmol of sodium would be equivalent to 230 mg urinary sodium.

**P-Creatinine**

P-Creatinine is measured in umol/l. The blood test is used as a marker for the kidney function to estimate eGFR. Additionally it is used for a measurement of GFR because it is a waste product of muscle metabolism produced at a constant rate, freely filtered and not reabsorbed. Creatinine is to a lesser extent secreted which could lead to overestimation of GFR [(Giebisch et al., 2017a, s. 741\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?S7JimO)

**P-Urea**

Is measured in mmol/l. The blood sample was collected in a Vacuette blood collection tube, mint prop 3,5 ml (Lithium-Heparin with gel). Amino acids derived from proteins are catabolized to urea in the liver and is the end product in the protein metabolism[(Giebisch et al., 2017b, s. 831\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?RbMcKz). Urea can be used as a marker for protein balance. Urea is also used as a clinical relevant end-point to determine the severity of the disease since urea accumulates as CKD progresses.

**P-Phosphorus**

Is measured in mmol/l. The blood sample was collected in a Vacuette blood collection tube, mint prop 3,5 ml (Lithium-Heparin with gel). Plasma phosphorus is used as a marker for phosphorus balance. The majority of the phosphorus content is located intracellular and in plasma a fraction is bound to plasma albumin and is therefore not a good indicator for total phosphorus balance. 

**P-Ionized calcium**

Is measured in mmol/l. The blood sample was collected in a Vacuette blood collection tube, gold prop 3,5 ml with gel. Ionized calcium is the free active form of calcium. Additionally calcium is also used as a buffer for protons in the state of metabolic acidosis which is a common problem for advanced CKD patients.

**P-PTH**

Parathyroid hormone (PTH) is measured in pmol/l and was collected in a Vacuatte blood collection tube.

**U-Phosphorus**

Urinary phosphorus was collected  using a 24- hour urine sample and represents renal excretion. Urinary phosphorus excretion is affected by dietary intake and GFR and was used together with dietary intake to calculate the apparent phosphorus balance. 

**U-Calcium**

Urinary calcium was collected  using a 24- hour urine sample and represents renal excretion. Urinary calcium is used for the primary end point (balance between excretion and intake). However even for the healthy population, urinary calcium can’t be used as a surrogate marker for intake[(Toren & Norman, 2005\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?72AY1b). In CKD the marker is even less reliable. First Urinary calcium is severely reduced in advanced stage CKD. Second: CKD often has elevated PTH, further altering excretion [(Spiegel & Brady, 2012\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0pHSzq). In this study no measurement of fecal ca was done so the total body calcium cannot be determined . 

**U-Urea**

Urinary urea was measured from 24 hour urine collections to estimate daily protein intake.. this objective biomarker is used to evaluate the accuracy of the protein intake estimated from the two dietary assessment methods. 

Urinary urea concentration (mmol/l) was multiplied by the 24 hour urine volume to calculate the daily excretion (mmol/day) because. Each urea molecule has two nitrogen atoms, the urinary  nitrogen excretion was calculated using the atomic weight of nitrogen (14g/mol) **UUN (g/day) \= urinary urea \[mmol/day\] x 2 x 14g)/1000.** 

 Estimated dietary protein intake (DPI) was calculated using the  Maroni equation . 

**DPI (g/day)=6.25×(UUN\[g/day\] \+ 0.032 x bodyweight \[kg\])** [(Wang et al., 2022\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ik0k2q).  

UUN represents urinary urea nitrogen. Total nitrogen excretion is by 6.25 as proteins contain approximately 16% nitrogen by weight [(Masud et al., 2002\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Qeb1dR).

**U-Creatinine**

Urinary creatine was collected using a 24 hour urine sample. Urine creatinine is used in this study to calculate GFR. eGFR used in clinical practice can overestimate kidney function compared to GFR calculated by the clearance of creatinine[(Lahiji et al., 2025\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?CFBO07). In addition, GFR calculated by creatinine clearance is less determined by muscle mass, compared to eGFR. Consequently, eGFR tends to overestimate kidney function in individuals with sarcopenia, conversely a bodybuilder would get a falsely low eGFR.  In this study GFR was calculated as  (U-creatinine x V)/(P-Creatinine x 1440 [(Giebisch et al., 2017a, s. 741\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?U5P6ll) where U-Crea is the urinary creatinine concentration, V is the total 24-hour volume, P-Crea is the plasma creatinine concentration and 1440 represents the total number of minutes in 24 hours.

**U-Sodium**

Urinary sodium was collected using a 24 hour urine sample. Urinary sodium excretion was compared to the sodium intake obtained by the dietary assessment methods. This method is unable to capture sodium excreted via fecal or sweat. In addition urinary sodium is affected by diurnal variations of aldosterone and cortisol. However this method is superior compared to spot urine for assessing sodium intake[(Afsar et al., 2025\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SHmY1v). In this study added table salt was not weighted or photographed by the participants. Aldosterone levels were not measured. It cannot be excluded that these factors affect the results. 

**Magnesium considerations**

Urinary magnesium was not collected but the estimated intake is reported. Magnesium intake can affect calcium uptake, magnesium may bind to CaSR as well and is regulated by PTH too [(Giebisch et al., 2017d, s. 791\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?fO6WFM). Magnesium can affect phosphate absorption, due to its properties as a divalent cation by binding phosphate and form insoluble complexes[(De Francisco et al., 2010\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?lg6PgD)

**U-Volume**

The urine volume was collected using a 24 hour urine sample. The urine volume is used to calculate the total excretion of the solutes by multiplying the volume in litres by the concentration of the solutes per litre. 

## **Categorizing of plants and animal sources** {#categorizing-of-plants-and-animal-sources}

To calculate the intake of plant and animal calcium and phosphorus from the photographs and the weighted records the two categorizations have been separated

All vegetable dishes were included in the plant calcium-phosphorus category, because of a higher ratio of plant to animal components in these dishes. The possibility of dairy and other animal products in these dishes are present and could potentially lead to inaccuracy in the analysis. Coffee and tea were  included in the water category  Cakes and sweets with a higher ratio of plant to animal phosphorus and calcium were included in the plant category. Cream cakes or any type of pastry or dessert with a higher animal to plant phosphorus or calcium fraction were included in the animal category. For calcium and sodium water were included in a category alone.

Phosphorus from food additives was not included in the analysis in this master thesis. Additives can be identified from the ingredient list, the exact phosphorus content is not available on the food labels. Therefore accurate quantification of phosphorus intake from additives was not possible to calculate. This may potentially lead to underestimation of total phosphorus intake. The fraction of plant and animal phosphorus and calcium were reported in % of total dietary intake excluding supplements. In addition to that, a fraction of specific food groups were reported. Food groups contributing to less than 5% of total phosphorus and calcium or food groups rarely consumed  \<30% of total n were excluded to identify the major calcium and phosphorus food groups. For calcium and phosphorus cold cuts, cured, smoked products, pate, sausages and meat dishes were included in the meat category. Fish products and shell fish were included in the fish category. Butter, milk, cream, yoghurt and any type of cheese were included in the dairy category.

## **Calcium content in drinking water** {#calcium-content-in-drinking-water}

In addition to the  calcium content in the water tracked by vitakost, It was decided to calculate the calcium content in drinking water based on the participants' topography. All participants' zip codes were  collected and stored in redcap. The calcium content was calculated based on the location. To calculate the calcium content a database was used to identify the hardness of water. [(*Vandets hårdhed i danske kommuner*, u.å.)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Ov3pPl). Water hardness  was extracted in German hardness degree (dH) by definition, 1 °dH  equals 10 mg of calcium oxide (CaO)  per liter, which is equal to 7.17 mg elemental calcium  per liter. Total calcium intake from drinking water was quantified using the following equation Daily calcium from water (mg/day) \= total water volume (l/day) X (water hardness °dH ) X 7.17 mg/L. [(*German Hardness \- Glossary \- ALMAWATECH*, u.å.)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?H2mWDz) . The calcium fraction from drinking water both reported by vitakost and by the modified approach was reported in % of total calcium content.

## **Intention to treat**  {#intention-to-treat}

By doing an intention to threat (ITT) analysis the participants are all analyzed regardless of dropout or protocol deviation. This method takes account for the practical clinical scenario because it admits non compliance and protocol deviations. ITT strengthens the external validity due to the analysis taking account of the real life situation and avoiding overestimating the effect size. The entire sample is included which keeps the power in the study. However participants with missing data can increase the risk of type II errors ie[(Gupta, 2011\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ysubWb)

## **Per protocol** {#per-protocol}

Per protocol (PP) analysis includes only participants that adhered to the study protocol ie. participants that have attended the baseline visit and completed the dietary registrations. In a PP analysis all participants who had not adhered to the study protocol would be excluded. In a case where two methods are compared, the PP analysis may show the actual difference while an ITT will underestimate the difference between the superior method and the inferior  [(Tripepi et al., 2020\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SRzuOO)

## **Spearman rank sum** {#spearman-rank-sum}

The spearman rank sum test is used for continuous or ordinal variables, non parametric data or data with relevant outliers. The test can be used as a measure of a monotonic correlation. The formula for spearman are symmetric (P=X,Y)  meaning the correlation between x and y is the same regardless of which column x and y is. All the variables are ranked according to the value of the variable in the data set. The difference and the square of the difference of the rank numbers of the variables is calculated. A correlation coefficient ranges from \-1 to \+1 whereby a perfect positive monotonic correlation gives \+1 and a perfect negative gives a \-1 and no monotonic correlation gives a correlation coefficient of 0\. [(Schober et al., 2018a)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?UKIRQ3) figure 3 illustrates the interpretation of R values. Spearman rank sum analysis was in this master thesis done for primary outcome for measuring the correlation between phosphorus and calcium balance and the clearance. In addition to that it was used for comparing the two dietary assessment methods.

## **Bland altman plot** {#bland-altman-plot}

Although a correlation test quantifies the linear association between two variables, it does not automatically imply there is a good agreement between methods. To evaluate the agreement between the two methods a bland Altman plot was used. A scatter plot is constructed by plotting the difference between the two methods on the y-axis against the corresponding mean value of the two methods on the x-axis. A mean bias value is calculated and this value can either be positive or negative, a positive value means one of the methods on average gives a higher value and a negative gives a lower value. Additionally the method calculates a 95% limit of agreement interval in which 95% of the differences between the two methods are expected to fall. [(Giavarina, 2015\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?d9tu1P)

## **Mann-Whitney U test** {#mann-whitney-u-test}

The Mann-Whitney U tests if there is a difference between two groups. This is determined by comparing the rank sum of two variables in contrast to the t-test which compares the mean between two samples. Mann-Whitney is the non parametric counterpart to the non paired t-test. The test can handle continuous or ordinal data because it works with ranks. The test works in three steps. First all data points from both groups are ranked from highest to lowest (1 for smallest, 2 for the next smallest). The rank sum of group A is compared to the rank sum of group B. Subsequently a U value is calculated if one of the groups has higher ranks consistently the U value will show a significant difference[(Chicco et al., 2025\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ZXTSRs). The test as used in this master thesis for secondary outcomes.

## **Willcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test for paired comparison.** {#willcoxon-matched-pairs-signed-rank-test-for-paired-comparison.}

This test is a non parametric test used to compare the median of two dependent paired samples [(Rosner et al., 2006\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ZLQyrY). It serves as the non-parametric equivalent to the paired Student's t-test.  To test for a significant difference there is a null hypothesis. H0= there is no difference between the dietary intake of day 1 and 2\. The H0 is accepted if the p value is \>0,05. The p value at this level will result in a 20% chance of type-II error and a 5% chance of a type I error at a test strength of 80%.

The difference is calculated between two variables and subsequently ranked based on the placement of the variable in the dataset. The difference between the rank sum of positive and negative rank numbers determines whether or not there is a significant difference. The Wilcoxon matched-pairs signed-rank test was used for primary and secondary endpoints regarding the comparison of the two methods for the same participant.

## **Subgroup analysis** {#subgroup-analysis}

Subgroup analysis was planned at first to compare the balance between  participants in CKD stage  5 with those at stage 3 and 4\. However because of a limited number of participants in stage 5 (n=3) the statistical power would be too low, an altered approach was done by stratifying the group into a subgroup with a creatinine clearance of  \<20ml/min (n=8) and a group of \>=20ml/min (n=23) 

* The advantage of this is to evaluate if there is a trend towards a positive phosphorus or sodium balance at a certain level of clearance.  
* However, stratifying into a subgroup with a lower number of participants (n=8) increases the risk of type II error (false negatives). The conduction of multiple testing increases the risk of type I error as well (false positives). Additionally extreme outliers increases the risk for type I errors.[(Brookes et al., 2001\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?yvFbCS)

**Sensitivity analysis**

In this study it was decided to do a sensitivity analysis to include the data available. This was done in addition to the primary analysis due to multiple participants with a missing dietary registration the day they collected their 24-hour urine. This approach reduces the risk of type II errors. However, multiple statistical testing increases the risk of type I errors and including dietary registrations that do not align with the day of 24-hour urinary collection day may alter the correlation. [(Mowbray et al., 2022\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?nPvw8M)

## **Missing data** {#missing-data}

Missing data is typically grouped into three categories: 

* Missing completely at random (MCAR) The data missing is independent of the observed and unobserved data. In the case of MCAR no systematic differences exist between the participants with missing data and those with complete data. This will reduce power and lead to type-II errors  but does not lead to bias. In this study it can be a missing laboratory value because a batch of lap samples was processed improperly[(Pedersen et al., 2017\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?1Y2cP9).  
* Missing at random (MAR).  In this situation data missing are systematically related to the observed data but not the unobserved data that are missing. In this study age or disease burden ie. GFR can be the observed data leading to missing data. Older participants may have a harder time filling out a three day weighted dietary registration or taking photographs of food and beverages.   
* Missing not at random (MNAR). The data missing is systematically related to the unobserved data. MNAR is difficult to handle because the data missing cannot be predicted based on the observed data alone. An example of MNAR in this study could be participants having a dietary pattern deviating from the recommendations ie. high phosphorus intake. The participant in this example may underreport the intake and this will lead to systematic difference since the missingness depends on the unobserved data.

# **Discussion** {#discussion}

## **Phosphorus balance** {#phosphorus-balance}

In this dietary study both dietary assessment methods failed to find a significant correlation between the apparent phosphorus balance and creatinine clearance, both in the primary and the sensitivity analysis. It is possible a larger sample size would have provided higher statistical power to detect a significant correlation between the excretion to intake ratio, and the creatinine clearance for the IBDA. Only 31 participants were available for the data analysis, compared to the estimated 42 required by the power calculation, a full sample size may potentially alter these findings. 

The available sample size yielded a weak correlation (r=0.31) but borderline significant p value of (p=0.087). However this should be interpreted with caution. First a weak correlation may not be clinically meaningful. Secondly, although the correlation between phosphorus intake assessed by the photographs and 24-hour urinary excretion was significant (p=0.03) the correlation was weak (r=0.39) which could indicate the validity of the method may be rather low. 

Although the image based method indicated this borderline trend towards a lower excretion to intake ratio of phosphorus as GFR declines. The weighted food record failed to show a significant correlation (r=0.24, p=0.19)  between the excretion to intake ratio and the clearance. This absence of correlation occurred despite the fact that the weighted food record demonstrated a stronger, and a fair significant correlation (r=0.43) between estimated dietary intake and 24-hour urinary excretion. Even in the absence of the outlier which increased the correlation from a fair correlation to a moderate and highly significant (r=0.61, p=0.0003) between intake and 24-hour urinary phosphorus excretion (see method comparison) despite this the correlation between balance ratio and clearance were unaltered. Therefore it is possible that the borderline trend observed from the image based dietary method is caused by the method's higher systematic measurement error. More importantly clinically, the correlation between the intake-excretion and clearance was shown to be negligible by any of the methods. There are several possible explanations. 

* First, the compensation in the fractional urinary phosphorus excretion by the remaining nephrons are still able to keep up even at this advanced stage of the disease likely by the increasing production of FGF23 and PTH.  Unfortunately No FGF 23 samples were available for this dietary sub study so it was not possible to assess an association between FGF 23 and phosphorus balance.   
* Secondly as the disease progresses and the uremic symptoms worsens, dietary intake may decrease. So, even if the excretion to intake ratio declines the decreasing dietary phosphorus intake will decline as well and may result in an unaltered absolute phosphorus retention (intake-excretion). To evaluate this possibility a spearman rank sum test was done to assess the correlation between dietary phosphorus intake and the creatinine clearance which failed to show a significant correlation for either of the methods. However Although not significant the median intake fell from 1078 in \<20 clearance group to 1040 mg in \>20 clearance group for weighted food records and even more markedly from 1074 to 943 assessed from photographs so it cannot be rejected that it may have partially masked a possible decline in renal excretion.   
* Thirdly it is possible that the skewed distribution in the GFR range has affected the results. Only 9% of the participants were classified as stage 5 vs 78% being stage 4 so it may have been different if the distribution were similar. However, given the substantial span in GFR across this sample (9-54) the fact that the correlation coefficient between the intake-excretion and clearance remained near zero suggests that a skewed distribution alone may not account for the lack of association. But existing studies suggest that the kidneys are still able to excrete a sufficient amount of phosphorus at stage 3-4 [(Salomo et al., 2017\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?PA1JbW) [(Misella Hansen et al., 2023\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SOfQ6t) [(Hill et al., 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?tYkg5h). The exact point where this compensatory mechanism fails is not fully understood and likely varies individually. 91% of the participants were either stage 3 or 4 so it is possible these participants have not yet reached the point where excessive phosphorus cannot be excreted and strict dietary phosphorus intake may be necessary. Dietary phosphorus restrictions seem to be beneficial for managing hyperphosphatemia in dialysis patients [(Guida et al., 2011\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kOQjLL). A Mann-Whitney test was performed to further explore this with a cut off \<20ml/min segregating the cohort into a group of  \<20ml/min (n=8) and a group of 23 \>20ml/min (n=23) . While no statistically significant difference was found the median excretion to intake ratio dropped from 57.8% (\>20ml/min) to 45.3% (\<20ml/min) for image based method and 51.2 % to 42.1 %  for weighted food record. It could be speculated that this lack of statistical significance is due to lack of power within the \<20 clearance group and that a different outcome would have emerged with a  higher proportion of stage 5 CKD participants, who are closer to  terminal renal failure.   
* Fourth, it cannot be excluded that the heterogeneity  in the underlying disease may have affected the way the nephron handles the phosphorus whether it is tubular damage or glomerular damage. Biologically it would make sense as roughly 85% of the filtered phosphorus is reabsorbed within the proximal tubule. Consequently an  underlying disease i.e. polycystic kidney disease which is manifested by progressive tubule damage [(Halvorson et al., 2010\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?yXrQLK) may alter phosphorus handling differently than type 2 diabetes which is more characteristically manifested by initial glomerular damage [(Tavafi, 2013\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?Ay26Zl).

It is important to mention some methodological considerations. A true phosphorus balance was not measured, as no fecal samples were collected so the phosphorus content in feces is not known. Consequently a true balance cannot be established by urinary data alone. Nevertheless, the few existing balance studies that  have examined fecal phosphorus content such as Hill et al (2013) demonstrate that the fractional absorption remains relatively stable at approximately 50-60% in CKD stage 3-4 mirroring healthy participants. In the present cohort the urinary excretion ratios in the \>20 clearance group align closely with this absorption rate. It is not known if the absorption was impaired in the \<20 clearance group but assuming it is stable the observed downward trend could indicate a shift towards retention. 

A single 24-hour phosphorus urinary sample may as well not be enough to get an accurate assessment of the balance; And a correlation does not determine the absolute intake in mg.

 It is suggested multiple 24-hour urinary samples are needed especially for patients with CKD where daily urinary phosphorus excretion may be highly variable [(Stremke et al., 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?ZDqtYv). In contrast Salomo et al found a single 24 hour urinary phosphorus sample was sufficient to estimate individual phosphorus excretion for patients with CKD stage 3-4 [(Salomo et al., 2017\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?xmqxSa). In this study the phosphorus intake estimated by both methods correlated with the 24-hour urine, especially the weighted record that showed a moderate and highly significant correlation after the outlier was excluded r=0.61 p=0.0003 (method comparison section). Interestingly this aligns closely with correlation found in studies with healthy individuals [(Morimoto et al., 2014\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?kiAWRV) . So one 24-hour urinary sample may have been sufficient to estimate a low vs high dietary intake of phosphorus

To further evaluate the validity of a single 24-hour collection a test was done to assess the correlation between 24-hour urinary phosphorus excretion and 24-hour urea excretion. A strong and highly significant correlation between urinary phosphorus excretion and urinary urea was found (r=0.83) (p=\<0.0001).  Because dietary phosphorus and protein are tightly coupled this could indicate the 24-hour urinary phosphorus sample was somehow representable. Urinary urea is validated as a urinary biomarker for dietary protein intake. However to estimate dietary intake by urinary urea it is required for the participant to be in a steady state [(Bingham, 2003\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?IZp86R). As the participants in this study were at advanced stages of CKD stage a steady state assumption must be applied cautiously. It is estimated by ESPEN 20-40% of patients with CKD stage 4-5 have protein energy wasting[(Wright et al., 2019\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?dJf4cY). Advanced uremia is associated with systemic inflammation and increased protein catabolism, meaning excreted nitrogen may partially reflect endogenous muscle breakdown and not dietary intake alone. In addition It cannot be rejected that the dietary intake from days prior to the urinary sample collection may have affected the 24-hour urinary excretion. One outlier had a 24-hour urinary phosphorus excretion of 852 mg exceeding their day 3 intake assessed by WFR (699 mg). At day 2 the participant had a phosphorus intake of  roughly 1400 mg–double the amount ingested on day 3\. The majority of the phosphorus intake were ultraprocessed sausages so it is likely the high bioavailability of the phosphorus additives may have caused a delayed renal clearance that spilled over into the 24-hour urinary phosphorus sample. Another thing to take into consideration is the inaccuracy of the different food databases.

The phosphorus content in vitakost is estimated and studies indicate a difference between the actual phosphorus content and expected, especially for food items with phosphorus additives, that could potentially underestimate a phosphorus retention across the entire sample  [(Sullivan et al., 2007\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?j2WS19).

## **Method comparison phosphorus** {#method-comparison-phosphorus-1}

Overall the weighted food record was shown to be superior compared to the image based dietary assessment when estimating dietary phosphorus intake. Meat products constituted a major source of phosphorus, contributing to more than 20% of the total intake. Quantifying  phosphorus from certain  meat types is particularly hard via photographs. 

Even though reference pictures exist of specific meat and fish, estimation of the raw or cooked weight remains difficult , meaning that minor measurement errors are inevitable.  Since meat is an abundant phosphorus source an overestimation or underestimation that are noticeable can largely affect the total phosphorus content thus the correlation. This was amplified for meat products that were completely lacking a reference picture, where portions had to be estimated using similar food matches, vitakost own standardized metric, or as a last resort, subjective estimation. However this was even more critical for products without a reference picture but at the same time contains an exceptional amount of phosphorus such as liver, cod roe and sweatbreads. Because of this high phosphorus density even minor under- or overestimation in the volume led to vast over- or underestimation in the phosphorus content on an individual level. 

In contrast other major phosphorus sources: dairy or cereal/bread products offered a higher degree of estimation. Most participants consumed either slices of cheese, milk or yoghurt. Estimating the weight of these three food groups is less difficult: there are reference pictures for both cheese and yoghurt and vitakost has standardized portions ie. regular slice of cheese=20g. For milk the estimation of the volume based on standard glass sizes was also kind of accurate. Similarly commonly consumed cold cuts are easier to portion estimate as the weight per slice proves to be rather consistent (eg= standard size of is roughly 15g, and salami=5 g). For rye bread a standard slice is roughly 45 g and experience shows a slight margin of estimation error (+- 10g) does not alter the total phosphorus calculation to a noticeable degree. 

However the challenge regarding estimation of phosphorus in meat and the particularly dense phosphorus food may explain the variance observed in the data. The Wilcoxon signed-rank test failed to show a significant difference in between the two methods. This lack of significance may have occurred because of individual overestimations and underestimations from estimating unreferenced food or complex meat portions counterbalanced each other.

The correlation between the two methods was strong and highly significant demonstrating that both methods rank participants similar regarding high and low phosphorus intake. Despite an underestimation of 76 mg by the image method the bland altman plot revealed a large LOA and certain observations with a large deviation alongside certain observations showing large individual deviations.This indicates that while image based dietary method can be used for phosphorus estimation on group level it is less accurate on individual level and  cannot be used interchangeable with WFR for individual phosphorus estimation. This may also explain why it had a markedly lower correlation with the 24-hour urinary sample.

## **Calcium balance** {#calcium-balance}

Similarly to the phosphorus balance no significant correlation was found for the balance and the clearance. Regardless of the method or whether the correlation was measured as balance ratio or intake-excretion and clearance the correlation coefficient remained near zero. Reaching a full sample size of 42 would likely not have yielded a significant correlation. Interestingly in contrast to phosphorus, dietary calcium intake assessed by either methods failed to correlate with 24-hour urinary calcium excretion in both the primary and sensitivity analysis. The correlation coefficients ranged from a negligible negative (r= \-0.08 to a negligible positive r= 0.11). This indicates that both methods fail to assess the calcium balance and that dietary calcium intake is not a strong marker for urinary calcium excretion. The median urinary calcium excretion was very low (35 mg) and largely unaffected by dietary intake. Interestingly a spearman correlation between urinary calcium excretion and clearance revealed a weak positive trend (r=0.30 p=0.09), indicating calcium excretion tends to fall as clearance falls. This suggests that the outcome might have been different with a wider clearance span.

Besides the clearance there could be several other explanations for this low urinary output. One third of the participants had a normal P-PTH while the rest had an elevated P-PTH. PTH may alter the calcium excretion by affecting the urinary reabsorption and calcium release from the bones. However the Mann-Whitney test performed did fail to show a significant difference in urinary calcium excretion between the high PTH and normal/low PTH group. So PTH level is likely not the explanation here.  It could be hypothesized that compromised urinary excretion is caused by decreased absorption at the intestinal level. Theoretically to maintain calcium balance when intestinal absorption is low  it would make sense to increase tubular reabsorption. Vitamin D deficiency already starts to manifest in the earlier stages, which compromises intestinal calcium absorption. Not surprisingly no correlation was found between plasma vitamin D (25-OH) and urinary calcium excretion. Plasma 25-OH may be less associated with calcitriol the active form since the kidneys ability to convert 25-oh to the active form is severely compromised. 

Vitamin D is responsible for the active uptake of calcium and existing studies indicate it works up to a certain level of intake after which passive paracellular transport becomes more prominent at increasing intakes. [(Sheikh et al., 1988\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?wPORBH). The median intake in this cohort was 750 mg assessed by weighted food record and 590 mg by image based respectively. It's possible this level was not sufficient to drive a noticeable paracellular transport making the absorption more dependent on vitamin D. 9 out of 32 participants consumed alfacalcidol. To test if alfacalcidol affected the urinary calcium excretion a Mann-Whitney test was performed. Interestingly a significantly higher urinary excretion (24 vs 42 mg, p=0.0181) was observed in the non-alfacalcidol group. However a significant difference in the clearance was found between the two groups (19.6 ml/min vs 25.7 ml, p=0.03) This lower clearance might explain  explain the decreased urinary calcium excretion in the alfacalcidol consuming group, as it probably represents patients with a more advanced disturbance in the mineral metabolism. 

While the impaired urinary calcium excretion may be primarily explained by the decreased absorption, another potential factor that might, to a lesser degree, contribute to the low urinary output is tissue retention

The balance study conducted by Hill et al found a similar urinary calcium excretion (50mg) in their CKD stage 3-4 participants as the participants when consuming a diet of 950 mg calcium. Interestingly they observed a slightly lower fractional calcium absorption in their group compared to healthy participants but the absolute absorption was unaltered. At 950 mg/day intake Hill et al found a neutral calcium balance because of an increased fecal excretion (800 mg). However at an intake of 2500 mg (calcium carbonate) Urinary excretion failed to increase and a positive balance of roughly (500 mg) was observed despite a large increase in fecal calcium (1900 mg). Similarly Spiegel findings indicate a neutral balance on 800 mg and markedly positive on 2000 mg. In both studies they suggested a part of it may have deposited extraskeletal. The participants in the present cohort possess an average clearance of roughly 10-15 ml/min lower than the participants in Hill et al study. One could expect the threshold for positive balance shifts downwards. However verifying a  retention is not possible without fecal samples. Furthermore the sample size of the two balance studies were 8 in the hill study and 12 (6 healthy and 6 CKD stage 3-4) in the spiegel study respectively, a strictly fixed calcium and phosphorus intake and a different study population meaning the  results has to be interpreted with caution and cannot be directly extrapolated. But the findings from this sub study indicate that calculating a calcium balance between intake and clearance is unreliable and urinary calcium is highly inaccurate in determining a high or low dietary calcium intake.  Fecal samples are needed to assess a correlation between calcium balance and clearance. 

## **Method comparison Calcium** {#method-comparison-calcium-1}

For calcium the correlation between the two dietary methods was very strong and highly significant. However despite this correlation, the Wilcoxon sign-rank test revealed a significant difference in calcium intake between the two methods. 

This discrepancy was hypothesized to stem from a substantial lower water intake assessed by the image based dietary method compared to the weighted food record. In this study calcium derived from water constituted 19% (142 mg) of total calcium intake for weighted food records and 9%(44 mg) in the image based method. To test this the Wilcoxon was performed regarding water intake and  identified a highly significant difference in the water intake between the two groups. When calcium from water was excluded from the analysis the Wilcoxon test no longer identified a significant difference in the total calcium between the methods. This suggests the underestimation is primarily driven by unreported water intake. 

This is further supported by the fact that more than half of the calcium came from dairy products. As previously established dairy products yield a high degree of estimation agreement between the methods. Meat contributed a negligible amount (2-3%) of calcium intake so in contrast to phosphorus large over and underestimation would not alter calcium calculation to a noticeable degree.

This could also explain the bland altman plot. The plot revealed a systematic underestimation of 138 mg. However in contrast to phosphorus the underestimation is more consistent at low and high dietary intakes. The majority of the observations is tightly clustered around the bias line. Because both the methods quantify the primary source of calcium (dairy), rather similar the individual variation is minimized. This suggests with accurate assessment of water intake the IBDA holds a potential to be used interchangeably with WFR for assessment of calcium intake.

## **Sodium balance** {#sodium-balance}

An unexpected trend towards a positive balance as clearance declines was found in this study. To explore if there was a tendency towards a specific level of clearance where this sodium balance manifests a Mann-Whitney U test was performed on the sodium balance (intake minus excretion) using a clearance cut off of 20ml/min.  For both the image based method and the weighted food record a statistically significant difference was observed between the two groups. In the group with a clearance of \>= 20ml/min (n=22)  the median sodium balance was negative (-930 mg for image based method and \-554 mg/day for WFR)..  Conversely the group with clearance of \<20 ml/min (n=8) had a positive retention (+425 mg for image based method and \+ 57 mg/day for WFR). 

The true extent of this retention could be underestimated as added salt could not be identified in the dietary registrations. Conversely a minor fraction (7-10%)  of unmeasured sodium is excreted via feces, sweat and saliva which could underestimate the sodium excretion[(Lucko et al., 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?0oyGn2). However, these minor extra renal losses are unlikely to alter these large differences to a noticeable degree.

This positive retention is an unexpected finding as sodium is tightly regulated by aldosterone. There could be several explanations for this: In advanced CKD inappropriate (RAAS) activation is frequently observed despite fluid overload, which could promote sodium retention [(Siragy & Carey, 2010\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?sqoNIb). However, the contribution of RAAS is unknown as RAAS was not measured.

Another more likely explanation is the decline in clearance: roughly 90% sodium is excreted through the kidneys, one might think at low levels the capacity to excrete dietary sodium is overridden, this could maybe explain the highly significant increase in sodium balance in the group below \<20. It cannot be rejected that dietary methods failed to accurately estimate sodium intake: Daily urinary sodium excretion is highly variable. The kidneys require several days to reach a steady state following fluctuations in dietary sodium intake. This may be further delayed in CKD. A single 24-hour urinary sample may partially reflect the delayed excretion of dietary sodium consumed the days prior to the urinary sample. However both methods correlated significantly with 24-hour urinary sodium which supports some validity. And despite a limited power in the \<20 clearance group (n=8) the difference was significant in this shift toward positive sodium balance with a decrease in clearance.

## **Method comparison sodium** {#method-comparison-sodium}

For sodium the image based method’s capability of ranking participants sodium intake was closer to the WFR than for what was observed for protein and phosphorus reflected by the 24-hour urine (r=0.42) vs (r=0.50). However The bland-altman plot revealed a systematic underestimation which increases at higher intakes and similar to phosphorus a wide LOA  with large scattered individual variations. Because of these large individual variations the image-based method may not be interchangeable with the WFR for sodium assessment.

## **Protein intake and urea excretion** {#protein-intake-and-urea-excretion}

Similar to phosphorus the weighted food record correlated markedly stronger with 24-hour urinary urea excretion than the image based method. This may partly be explained by the fact that phosphorus and protein are closely linked in most food. The difficulty in estimating the phosphorus content in certain meat products will lead to the same estimation errors for protein. However the image based method seems to assess protein intake more accurately than phosphorus. While the phosphorus to protein ratio remains relatively stable (roughly 10:1) across the majority of meat products consumed like: steak meat, pig meat, poultry and even cold cuts specific items in contrast to the formerly mentioned cod roe or sweatbreads (ratio \> 20\) that are difficult to estimate and like dairy products possess a very high ratio. Although dairy products proved to be rather accurate to quantify, even a small estimation error will result in a markedly larger estimation in the phosphorus content compared to the protein content. This sensitivity to high ratio food likely explains the individual variations being larger in the phosphorus plot than protein. Additionally this may also explain the significant difference found for protein intake (but not phosphorus) captured by the Wilcoxon test. For protein The bland altman plot revealed both over and underestimation by the image based method similar at higher and lower intakes but the majority was underestimation, because this underestimation was uniform and consistent. Despite an average systematic underestimation of 6 g are rather small, because of the large LOA and individual over and underestimation the image based method may not be used interchangeably with the WFR on the individual level. Assessing dietary protein intake in relation to recommendations is unreliable if the method under and overestimation on individual level. 

**Secondary outcomes**

No deeper analysis was made for magnesium, dietary fibers, carbohydrates, energy and fat. However the results indicate the image based method tends to underestimate the dietary intake (except for dietary fibers) whereby the underestimation of magnesium intake was highly significant. This further supports a consistent underestimation of the image based dietary assessment on group level. 

Interestingly for the food group analysis no significant difference (except for water) was in the absolute intake of the major phosphorus, calcium and sodium sources between the two methods. This indicates the image based method is able to identify major contributing food sources for these specific micronutrients further supporting the method is useful on group level. Similarly for dietary guidelines the two methods the number of participants following phosphorus, calcium, sodium and protein guidelines were remarkably similar, but this has to be interpreted with caution as dietary guidelines are a specific cut off so even a large range of dietary intake could be within this cutoff. However this could further support that the method proves useful on group level as dietary guidelines are group level recommendations. 

## **Strength and limitations** {#strength-and-limitations}

This study has several strengths and limitations. A major strength is the high overall with the majority of participants completing both the dietary registrations and 24-hour urinary collection. Two dietary assessment methods to evaluate the balance. The significant correlations achieved between the dietary intake and an objectively 24-hour urinary biomarker serves as a major validation strength of the methods used for assessing correlation between balance and clearance. Finally the validation of the image based method against the weighted food record.

There are several other limitations than the previously mentioned. 

**Study design and participants characteristics**

The observational design has its limitations when exploring a phosphorus and calcium balance against creatinine clearance. The high heterogeneity in this sample including variations in the underlying cause of the CKD, medication, age, smoking status and other potential confounders was not statistically controlled for. Participants had unstandardized dietary intake of calcium, phosphorus and magnesium. At the intestinal level both calcium and magnesium can form insoluble complexes with phosphorus altering bioavailability and the urinary excretion. Similarly dietary fiber intake may also affect calcium absorption at the intestinal level. Magnesium may also regulate calcium balance by interactions with CASR and PTH. The ratio of these specific minerals were not controlled for and may act as a confounding factor [(Rodríguez-Ortiz et al., 2014\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?5sfCyK).  To explore a true balance a strictly balance study with a standardized dietary intake and fecal collection would be required. However such a design is expensive and invasive for participants, making it unfeasible for a larger sample size. This design however still allows for exploring associations. For validating the image based method this design is appropriate. Importantly the distribution of major known etiologies – specifically type 2 diabetes and hypertension – mimic other CKD groups. However it is a clear limitation that only three participants were classified as CKD stage 5\. This limits the extrapolation of the results to a terminal CKD group.

**Selection bias and participant burden**

Selection bias must be acknowledged. Some of the patients eligible for the study chose not to participate because of the high burden of weighing and photographing all food and beverages. In this study the younger and more functioning participants tended to most accurately capture their dietary intake.The majority of the participants were CKD stage 4 with only three being stage 5\. This weakens the external validity as it cannot be extrapolated to participants having end stage renal disease.

Requiring participants to conduct two parallel dietary assessment methods simultaneously is demanding and likely resulted in a higher participant burden. Some participants may not have viewed the two methods as strictly independent which could have led to omitted photographs because they already recorded the weight.

Participants only attended one information meeting where they received instructions for dietary registration. Without subsequent practical training sessions, it is possible one information meeting was insufficient for some of the participants to record properly.

**Type I and Type II errors**

The study fell short of the 42 participants estimated by the power calculation. To increase the statistical power and reduce the risk of type II errors the sensitivity analysis was done. Consequently this also increased the risk for type I errors. In research one could argue a type I error is more detrimental than type II errors; falsely concluding something can lead to a misinformed conclusion rather than no conclusion, therefore it is important to address. It's possible the sample size for sensitivity analysis (n=31) is insufficient to detect a significant correlation due to lack of statistical power.  The lack of power becomes even more prominent in sub group analysis, it was not possible to make a comparison of balance in relation to clearance between stage 4 and 5 participants since only three participants were stage 5 (\<15ml/min clearance). While shifting the cut off to\<20ml/min provided a sub group of (n=8) this analysis was likely still limited in power potentially increasing the risk of type II errors.. The risk of type I errors must be carefully evaluated as well. The multiple statistical test across various nutrients and subgroups without adjusting for this multiple testing increases the risk of type I errors. By utilizing a significance level of a=0.05, there is a 5% risk of finding significant difference by chance for every test conducted. Additionally It cannot be rejected when doing small subgroup analysis that any observed significance could be driven by individual outliers. 

**Other forms of bias**

The visual estimation of the portion sizes was performed by a single RD without cross validation by a secondary independent dietitian. This lack of inter-rater reliability testing lead to a potential single observer bias, since the estimation of portion size rely on one individual's subjective visual perception [(Mahtani et al., 2018\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?WWduzi)

 In a minority of the photographs, participants included the digital display of their food scales. It's difficult for the investigator to remain blinded to the weight scale when visually estimating the portion size. This may introduce an anchoring bias [(Sánchez Ordóñez et al., 2025\)](https://www.zotero.org/google-docs/?SncZtz) as potentially making the correlation between the methods stronger.

**Clinical application**

Despite having a lower accuracy than the WFR, the image based method correlated significantly with urinary sodium and protein indicating the method succeeded to rank a low and high dietary intake. Consequently in clinical practice where a WFR may be unfeasible due to the participant burden the image based method could serve as an alternative that could reduce recall bias and more accurately assess portion sizes compared to the 24-hour dietary recall. As this is the first time the method has been used to assess phosphorus and calcium intake in this participant group the availability of reference pictures for phosphorus and calcium specific foods are limited and could be further optimized in the future. The method could potentially be used for strengthen the WFR in situations where the WFR is demanding e.g. eating at restaurants

# **Conclusion** {#conclusion}

The purpose of this study was to assess the correlations between creatinine clearance and the balance (intake minus urinary excretion) as well as excretion to intake ratio of calcium and phosphorus. Additionally for the first time in this patient group to compare the dietary intake of phosphorus, calcium, sodium and protein using an image based dietary method against a weighted food record. Regarding the balances no significant correlations were found between  the creatinine clearance and the absolute balance or the excretion to intake of calcium and phosphorus. However, a significant trend towards sodium retention was identified as renal function declines. Specifically participants with a clearance of \<20ml/min exhibited a positive sodium balance. 

Highly significant and strong correlations were observed between the two dietary assessment  methods for the evaluated nutrients. The image based method generally resulted in systematic underreporting for all the evaluated nutrients. Regarding protein and especially phosphorus and sodium the method did show a large individual variation. Consequently, the two methods cannot be used interchangeably if the primary purpose is to accurately assess dietary intake. Conversely for calcium despite an overall systematic underreporting the image based method could potentially be used interchangeably with the WFR If a standardized correction factor for unrecorded water is applied.

# **Future perspectives** {#future-perspectives}

Although a true balance could not be quantified, the lack of correlation found in this study between phosphorus balance and clearance, suggest that there is no real trend towards an increasing retention as renal function declines within this specific cohort. This indicates that participants even at this stage of CKD (median clearance 25ml/min), manage to compensate and excrete the dietary phosphorus. This trend aligns with the few existing studies done on stage 3-4 CKD participants, where changes in dietary phosphorus intake have been shown to be reflected by shift in the urinary phosphorus excretion to maintain balance. The threshold where the compensation fails was not identified in this study. Therefore for future studies it would be relevant to do a study with a larger fraction of stage 5 participants with a true balance

Additionally the results from this study suggesting a calcium balance solely based on urinary excretion and dietary intake cannot be established. To identify a retention future studies must include fecal samples. Especially in this CKD group where the upper limit of calcium is even less known. 

Another important finding was the positive sodium balance observed at lower clearance levels. For further evaluation of this, a future study could compare patients in different stages of advanced CKD (e.g. stage 4 vs 5\) during a habitual diet and a controlled sodium diet.

Methodologically while this study suggests the image-based dietary method cannot be used interchangeably to the WFR  assessing the dietary intake of phosphorus, sodium and protein. The method is less invasive and demanding for a participant. The detailed degree of quantifying food volume is higher than the 24-hour recall and the method is less prone to recall bias. Interestingly the method performed surprisingly well for calcium. Since the underreporting of fluids may be due to the participant finding it burdensome to photograph every single glass of water, instructions based on this could optimize this. Potentially by expanding the food database with more specific reference portion sizes for

 phosphorus, protein and sodium the quantification accuracy could be further improved.
